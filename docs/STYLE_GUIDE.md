# Writing for ages 7–12

Use short, everyday language. The player should be able to read an alert, compare its choices, and understand what changed.

1. Aim for about 15 words per sentence and one idea per sentence.
2. Use familiar words such as **air**, **sand**, and **solar flash**.
3. Explain unfamiliar terms where they first appear. A **sol** is one day on Mars. **Radiation** is invisible energy that can hurt the crew.
4. Use descriptions children can compare. Mars gravity is a little over one third of Earth’s gravity. Keep technical measurements in the linked sources.
5. Address the player as **you** or **your crew**. Keep the tone calm during a rescue. Earth sends a ship to bring the crew home.
6. Use the **Why?** card to connect a choice to a real astronaut, mission, or scientific fact. Add its source to [Data sources](DATA_SOURCES.md).
7. Make the cost clear in the outcome. “The repair worked, but it used some of your suit’s air.”
8. Keep the meaning and variables aligned in all five languages. Preserve placeholders such as `{nome}` and `{n}`.

| Situation | Example in the game’s style |
| --- | --- |
| A solar particle event is approaching | “Particles from the Sun are coming! Time to get to the shelter.” |
| Dust reduces panel output | “The panels are covered in dust. They make less power today.” |
| Low gravity affects bones | “Bones can get weaker in low gravity. That’s why astronauts exercise.” |

## Shared terms

- **Sol** means a Martian day, about 24 hours and 40 minutes.
- **Solar storm** means the Sun sends out energy and fast-moving particles.
- **Sand shield** means the layers of sand the robot puts over the base.
- **Shelter** means the most protected room at the outpost.
